\begin{abstract}
Self-supervised foundation models for surgical video currently follow two 
competing paradigms: pixel-reconstruction objectives and latent-predictive 
objectives, which move the prediction target into the encoder's own 
representation space. It remains unclear how much of the reported 
advantage of latent methods is due to the objective itself rather than 
the engineering scaffolding that typically accompanies it. This thesis 
isolates the contribution of the objective by training two surgical 
foundation models, General Surgery Vision Transformer (pixel-based) and LeJEPA (latent-based), under 
matched data and compute budgets on a curated corpus of CATARACTS, 
JIGSAWS, and PitVis, and evaluating their frozen representations on 
Cholec80 surgical phase recognition through layer-wise linear probing 
and UMAP visualization. LeJEPA outperforms GSViT in most layers, with a 
clear emergence zone at mid-depth layers where probe scores peak and 
cross-fold variance is smallest, while GSViT produces a flat, 
near-uniform profile across all layers. UMAP projections corroborate 
these findings: LeJEPA embeddings form progressively richer, 
phase-coherent manifold structure with depth, whereas GSViT remains an 
amorphous cloud at every layer. These results suggest that a minimal 
latent-predictive recipe, requiring only a single distributional 
regularizer and no auxiliary heuristics, produces surgical 
representations that are more semantically robust than those from pixel 
reconstruction, and that the choice of pretraining objective is a 
primary determinant of representation quality for surgical phase 
recognition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Surgery is one of the highest-stakes domains in modern medicine. An estimated 330 million major surgical procedures are performed worldwide each year \parencite{mascagni-2022}, and roughly half of all serious adverse events in hospitals occur in the operating room, with a large fraction of these events considered preventable \parencite{Sameera2021Human}. Minimally invasive procedures, in particular endoscopic and laparoscopic surgeries, have become the standard of care for a growing range of interventions because they reduce trauma, shorten recovery times, and lower the risk of post-operative complications compared to open surgery \parencite{buia-2015}. These benefits, however, come at a cost for the surgeon. The operative scene is no longer observed directly but through a narrow camera feed, depth perception is reduced to a two-dimensional projection, and the surgeon must orient themselves in an environment that is small, glossy, and frequently obscured by smoke, blood, and specular reflections from the light source. These systems matter clinically. If a model can tell which phase of an operation is happening, which instruments are in use, and how they touch the tissue, it can support several useful tools: software that warns the team when the operation deviates from the expected workflow, objective skill assessments for trainee surgeons, automatic logging of what happened during a case. All of these tools depend on the same thing: a vision system that can pull meaningful structure out of surgical video. The quality of that representation sets the ceiling for everything built on top of it, which is why this thesis focuses on it.

The dominant strategy in modern computer vision for producing such representations is self-supervised pretraining of large foundation models \parencite{new-era-in-vision-2025}. Instead of training a separate supervised model for every task, a single encoder is pretrained on large quantities of unlabelled video and then reused, frozen or lightly fine-tuned, across many downstream problems. Two families of self-supervised objectives currently compete for this role. Pixel-based methods train the encoder to reconstruct the raw input, either directly as in masked autoencoders \parencite{he-2021} or through forward video prediction as in GSViT \parencite{schmidgall2024general}. Latent-based methods, by contrast, move the prediction target out of pixel space and into the encoder's own representation space. Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures \parencite{LeCun2022APT}, V-JEPA \parencite{assran-2025}, and the recent surgical model UniSurg \parencite{wu-2026} are representative examples. The shift is motivated by a simple observation: in surgical video, most pixel-level variation, smoke, blood texture, specular highlights, and camera noise, is irrelevant to what a downstream task ultimately needs, and a model trained to reconstruct these details spends its capacity on the wrong things \parencite{wu-2026}.

Latent objectives, however, have historically been difficult to optimise. They admit a trivial solution in which the encoder maps every input to the same constant vector, a failure mode known as representation collapse, and the heuristics typically used to prevent it, exponential moving average teachers, stop-gradient updates, asymmetric teacher-student designs, and multi-term auxiliary losses, introduce a large set of interacting hyperparameters that make training fragile and expensive \parencite{wagner-2022, maes-2026}. UniSurg, the current state of the art for latent surgical pretraining, achieves strong results but only by combining 3,658 hours of surgical video with motion-guided masking, spatiotemporal affinity self-distillation, and a feature-diversity regularizer \parencite{wu-2026}. It is therefore not clear how much of UniSurg's quality is due to the latent objective itself and how much is due to its accumulated engineering scaffolding. The recently proposed LeJEPA framework \parencite{balestriero-2025} offers a clean way to ask this question: it is the first JEPA that trains stably with a minimal recipe of one prediction loss and a single distributional regularizer, the Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularizer, and it dispenses entirely with teacher networks and stop-gradients.

This thesis uses LeJEPA and GSViT as matched representatives of the latent and pixel paradigms, and asks what each of them actually learns when trained under identical data and compute budgets on surgical video. Concretely, both encoders are pretrained on a curated corpus drawn from CATARACTS, JIGSAWS, and PitVis, and their frozen representations are evaluated on Cholec80 phase recognition. Two complementary tools are used to characterise the representations. Layer-wise linear probing reveals at which depth each encoder makes surgical-phase information linearly accessible, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection \parencite{mcinnes-2018} provides a qualitative view of how phase classes are arranged in the embedding geometry of each layer. This setup turns the comparison between pixel and latent pretraining into a controlled, layer-resolved measurement rather than a benchmark-level summary, and it isolates the contribution of the objective itself from the cascade of heuristics that surrounds it in UniSurg-style models.

The technical contribution of this work is therefore threefold. First, it provides the first side-by-side, layer-resolved comparison of a pixel-based and a latent-based surgical foundation model trained under matched conditions, controlling for data scale, architecture family, and compute budget. Second, it adapts the LeJEPA recipe to the surgical domain and shows that the minimal one-regularizer objective transfers stably to a small, sensitive, domain-shifted corpus, without the auxiliary heuristics that current surgical latent models rely on. Third, it combines quantitative probing with manifold visualization to produce a transparent account of the representational geometry each objective induces, an angle that the surgical foundation model literature has so far left largely unexamined.

The societal relevance of this comparison goes beyond the technical question. Cholec80 phase durations are heavily skewed, with a small number of long phases dominating the frame count and several short but clinically critical phases occupying only brief windows. Pixel-based pretraining, as the results in this thesis show, structurally allocates more of its capacity to the common phases and performs disproportionately worse on the rarer ones; latent pretraining with a distribution-matching regularizer does not. In short, the choice of pretraining objective determines whether a downstream surgical AI system reliably recognizes the brief, high-risk transitions of an operation or only its long, low-risk segments. Because every patient is different, and because workflow-aware support is most useful precisely at the rarer, harder moments of an operation, this is not a neutral implementation detail. The remainder of the thesis develops this comparison in full, starting with the relevant literature in section \ref{sec:related}.

\section{Related Work} \label{sec:related}

\subsection{Pixel-based Vision Foundational Models}
Recent advances in machine learning have shifted the computer vision field from specialized, supervised models toward general-purpose foundation models (FMs) trained with self-supervised learning \parencite{new-era-in-vision-2025}. The dominant approach in this domain is masked image modeling, popularized by MAE \parencite{he-2021}: a large fraction of input patches is masked, and an encoder-decoder is trained to reconstruct the missing pixels. This principle was extended to the temporal domain by VideoMAE \parencite{tong-2022}, which reconstructs spatiotemporal masks across video frames, and was further scaled by VideoMAE~V2 \parencite{wang-2023} through a dual masking strategy that enables training of billion-parameter video transformers. However, predicting in pixel space is inherently limiting: the model spends its capacity on low-level visual details that are largely irrelevant to downstream understanding (e.g., the smoke produced during a surgical procedure \parencite{Zhang2022A}), rather than on the semantic structures that matter, such as how an instrument interacts with a tissue \parencite{wu-2026}. This thesis uses GSViT, a pixel-reconstruction model, as the representative of this paradigm in order to test whether this capacity allocation problem manifests in the layer-wise structure of surgical representations.

\subsection{Latent-based Vision Foundational Models}
To address this limitation, a complementary line of work shifts the predictions from pixel space to the latent space \parencite{LeCun2022APT}. A widely used image-domain example is DINO \parencite{caron-2021}, which learns general-purpose visual features through self-distillation: a student network is trained to match the embeddings produced by a momentum (EMA) teacher. Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) \parencite{LeCun2022APT} take this idea one step further by introducing an explicit predictor that maps a context embedding to the latent representation of a masked target produced by a teacher encoder. The methodology has recently been used widely in planning and computer vision \parencite{NEURIPS2024_9c3828ad,fei-2023, bardes-2023, abdelfattah-2024, larey-2026, munim-2026}. V-JEPA \parencite{assran-2025} instantiates this paradigm for video and, like DINO, relies on an EMA teacher and stop-gradient updates on the teacher branch to prevent representation collapse. Because the loss is computed in latent rather than pixel space, V-JEPA acts as an implicit filter against low-level noise and instead captures the semantic structure and temporal dynamics that are crucial for video understanding \parencite{wu-2026}. However, achieving these strong representations requires a careful combination of training heuristics. EMA teachers \parencite{grill-2020, caron-2021}, stop-gradient updates \parencite{chen-2021}, masking schemes \parencite{zhou-2022, baevski-2022}, and multi-term auxiliary losses \parencite{oquab-2023} are typically needed in concert to prevent representation collapse \parencite{maes-2026}. This significantly increases training complexity, introduces a large set of interacting hyperparameters, and makes the optimization process fragile and difficult to reproduce \parencite{wagner-2022}. To address this fragility, \textcite{balestriero-2025} introduce LeJEPA, the first JEPA that trains stably end-to-end from raw pixels with a minimal recipe. Its objective consists of only a mean-squared error prediction loss and a single regularizer, the Sketched-Isotropic-Gaussian Regularizer (SIGReg), which enforces that latent embeddings follow an approximately isotropic Gaussian distribution and provably prevents collapse. This removes the need for EMA teachers, stop-gradient tricks, and frozen pretrained encoders, while keeping the architecture symmetric and free of the asymmetric heuristics that characterize prior latent methods. This thesis adopts LeJEPA rather than V-JEPA or DINO as the latent representative precisely because its minimal recipe isolates the effect of the latent objective itself from the engineering scaffolding that surrounds it in other methods.

\subsection{Surgical Vision Foundational Models}
Self-supervised foundation models for surgical video have evolved along the same pixel-to-latent trajectory described above, but under the additional constraints of a small and sensitive data domain. Early efforts in surgical computer vision concentrated on static frame analysis. Models such as EndoNet \parencite{endonet-2016} introduced supervised CNN backbones for tool-presence detection and surgical-phase recognition on Cholec80, establishing the basic benchmarks but ignoring the temporal dimension that is central to surgery \parencite{wu-2026}. The first surgical foundation models appeared in 2023: Endo-FM \parencite{wang-2023} adapted DINO-style self-distillation to a video transformer trained on roughly five million endoscopic frames, while SurgVLP \parencite{yuan-2023} aligned surgical clips with narrated lecture transcripts via a contrastive vision--language objective. Both placed the learning signal in the latent space, but neither explicitly modelled the forward dynamics of the surgical scene. GSViT \parencite{schmidgall2024general} was the first surgical foundation model to do so, pretraining an EfficientViT encoder on 680 hours of laparoscopic and robotic surgery spanning 28 procedures via forward video prediction. The objective, however, is defined in pixel space through an asymmetric decoder. As a consequence, the encoder spends much of its capacity modelling low-level visual detail such as specular highlights, smoke, blood texture, and camera noise, rather than the surgical semantics that downstream tasks actually require. UniSurg \parencite{wu-2026} brought the V-JEPA latent-prediction paradigm to the surgical domain. Trained from scratch rather than fine-tuned from a general-domain encoder, UniSurg learns surgical physical dynamics directly, without inheriting the biases of natural video pretraining. Achieving this stability, however, requires both an immense aggregation of data (UniSurg-15M, totalling 3{,}658 hours of surgical video across 13 anatomical regions) and a collection of domain-specific heuristics, including motion-guided masking, spatiotemporal affinity self-distillation, and a feature diversity regularizer. The resulting model is strong but expensive to train and architecturally heavy. This thesis does not attempt to replicate UniSurg's scale or its full heuristic stack. Instead, it pairs GSViT with the LeJEPA framework \parencite{balestriero-2025}, which offers a route to latent surgical pretraining at a fraction of the data and compute budget and without the cascade of asymmetric tricks that UniSurg inherits from V-JEPA. By training both models under matched conditions, the comparison isolates the effect of the pretraining objective from confounds such as data scale, architecture family, and auxiliary engineering.

\subsection{Research Gap} While UniSurg and LeJEPA both establish competitive architectures for latent surgical pretraining, the literature still lacks a deep, comparative understanding of what these latent spaces actually encode. In particular, it remains unclear whether models of this kind rely more heavily on localized, high-fidelity spatial features or on broader temporal dynamics to represent surgical workflow, and how this balance differs between pixel-based pretraining (GSViT) and latent-based pretraining (LeJEPA). To address this gap, this project systematically probes both models layer by layer and complements quantitative probing with UMAP visualizations of their internal representations, providing a transparent account of how surgical foundation models process spatio-temporal information.

\section{Methods: Pixel-based vs Latent-based representation}
This chapter operationalises the central research question, namely to 
what extent a latent-predictive objective improves the semantic 
robustness and topological organization of surgical phase 
representations compared to traditional pixel-reconstruction approaches. 
The question is decomposed into two sub-questions, each addressed by a 
distinct methodological component. SQ1 asks how the choice of 
pre-training objective affects the clustering quality of surgical phase 
embeddings; this is investigated qualitatively through the per-layer 
UMAP projections of Section~\ref{sec:umap}, which expose the topological 
organization of the embedding space without any class supervision. SQ2 
asks how the objective, latent future prediction versus pixel 
reconstruction, impacts the performance of the learned representation on 
a downstream task; this is investigated quantitatively through the 
layer-wise linear probing of Section~\ref{sec:probing}, which measures 
how linearly decodable surgical phase information is at each depth. 
GSViT and LeJEPA serve as the pixel-reconstruction and latent-predictive 
instantiations respectively, trained under matched data and compute 
budgets so that observed differences are attributable to the objective 
rather than to confounds.

The pretraining corpus was curated from popular data sources in endoscopic and laparoscopic surgeries. Four different datasets were curated into one big dataset: Cholec80 \parencite{endonet-2016}, CATARACTS \parencite{ac97-8m18-21}, JIGSAWS \parencite{Gao2014JIGSAWS}, PitVis \parencite{das-2025}. Cholec80 is the dataset used for evaluation of our two models learned representation. It is a dataset that contains 80 endoscopic videos of cholecystectomy surgeries \parencite{endonet-2016}. From the videos, each frame is extracted with 1 fps, and each frame was annotated with both instrument presence and with the current phase (e.g. Preparation, ClippingCutting) \parencite{endonet-2016}. The rest of the dataset were used for training GSViT \parencite{schmidgall2024general} and LeJEPA \parencite{balestriero-2025}. The CATARACTS dataset consists of 50 videos in total of cataract surgeries. It consists of two types of videos, of each surgery done on a patient: a microscopic and a tray video. Unlike the other three datasets, JIGSAWS does not contain footage of ongoing surgeries; it instead captures eight surgeons of varying skill levels performing three elementary surgical tasks: suturing, needle-passing, and knot-tying \parencite{Gao2014JIGSAWS}. It is included to expose the model to fine-grained human motion patterns characteristic of surgical gestures. Lastly, the PitVis dataset has 25 endoscopic pituitary surgery videos. To create an equal representation of each surgery, a maximum of 1 million frames were used. The data was split randomly.

\subsection{General Surgery Vision Transformer}
General Surgery Vision Transformer (GSViT) is built on the EfficientViT architecture \parencite{liu-2023}, which combines several optimization techniques to achieve a favourable accuracy-efficiency trade-off: a sandwich layout, Cascaded Group Attention (CGA), and a parameter reallocation strategy (see Fig.~\ref{fig:GSViT_arc}). The \textit{sandwich layout} reduces the number of self-attention layers per block by surrounding each attention layer with multiple MLP layers, lowering memory and compute cost since self-attention is the most expensive operation in a transformer block. \textit{Cascaded Group Attention} splits the input features along the channel dimension and routes each group to a separate attention head, avoiding the head redundancy of standard multi-head attention in which every head operates on the full feature map and the learned projections tend to be highly correlated. Within a block, the heads are additionally arranged in a cascade, so that later heads refine the representation produced by earlier ones and the effective depth of the attention stack increases without adding parameters. The \textit{parameter reallocation} strategy redistributes channel width across the network: modules that contribute most to representation quality, such as the query and key projections and the MLP expansion, are widened, while modules with diminishing returns, such as the value projections in deeper stages, are narrowed. This yields a higher-capacity allocation under the same overall parameter budget, with a measurable gain in inference speed. To reconstruct high-resolution images from the encoded representations, GSViT pairs the EfficientViT encoder with an asymmetric decoder \parencite{schmidgall2024general}. The encoded representation is first projected into a higher-dimensional space by a fully connected layer with batch normalization and a Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activation. A sequence of transposed convolutional (Conv) layers then progressively upsamples the feature maps to the original spatial resolution, with each layer followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation. Residual blocks with Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) attention modules at two different scales are interleaved between the transposed convolutions, recalibrating channel-wise feature responses to emphasize the features most relevant for reconstruction. The formal definitions of the sandwich layout and CGA are given in Appendix~\ref{app:a}.

\subsection{LeJEPA Model}
LeJEPA builds on the JEPA framework for self-supervised latent representation learning, but discards the auxiliary heuristics, stop-gradients, exponential moving averages, and teacher--student designs, that earlier JEPA variants rely on to avoid representation collapse. A single distributional regularizer, SIGReg, is sufficient to keep training stable \parencite{balestriero-2025}. The architecture comprises two modules: an encoder and a predictor. Given a frame $\mathrm{f}_t$, the encoder $enc_\theta$, instantiated as a Vision Transformer (ViT) \parencite{dosovitskiy-2020}, maps the input into a compact latent representation $\mathrm{z}_t$. The predictor $pred_\phi$ then models the dynamics of the environment in latent space, forecasting the embedding of the next frame from a window of $N$ past embeddings. For the encoder the project adopts the ViT-Tiny variant: $12$ transformer blocks with $3$ attention heads, a hidden dimensionality of $192$, and a patch size of $16$. The observation embedding $\mathrm{z}_t$ is read off from the \texttt{[CLS]} token of the final transformer block and then routed through a projection head, implemented as a single linear layer followed by Batch Normalization \parencite{ioffe-2015}. This projection is necessary because the ViT architecture applies a Layer Normalization \parencite{ba-2016} at its last layer, which constrains the geometry of the \texttt{[CLS]} representation in a way that is incompatible with SIGReg's distributional matching objective. Remapping into a fresh representation space restores the freedom that SIGReg needs in order to act on the embeddings. The predictor is a deeper transformer model \parencite{vaswani-2017}: $6$ layers, $16$ attention heads, $10\%$ dropout. It takes the past $N$ frame representations and predicts the next embedding autoregressively, with temporal causal masking enforcing that no future embedding can leak into the prediction of an earlier one. The predictor's output passes through its own projection head, identical in design to the encoder's, before the loss is computed. Encoder, predictor, and both projection heads are optimized jointly under the LeJEPA objective.

\paragraph{Training Objective.} The LeJEPA objective combines two terms: a prediction loss that drives accurate forecasting in latent space, and a regularization loss that prevents the encoder from collapsing onto a trivial solution. The prediction loss is computed under teacher forcing as the squared Euclidean distance between the predictor's output and the encoder's embedding of the actual next frame. Optimizing this term in isolation is insufficient, as it admits a degenerate solution: the encoder can drive the loss to zero by mapping every input to the same constant vector, since a constant sequence is trivially predictable. This failure mode, known as representation collapse, is the issue that earlier JEPA variants address through stop-gradients, momentum encoders, and asymmetric teacher--student designs. LeJEPA addresses collapse with a single distributional constraint. SIGReg requires the embeddings produced over a batch to follow an isotropic Gaussian distribution, which excludes the degenerate solution by construction: a constant solution concentrates all probability mass at one point and cannot match a Gaussian target. Because classical normality tests degrade in high dimensions, SIGReg evaluates Gaussianity through random one-dimensional projections of the embeddings, relying on the Cram\'er--Wold theorem, which states that two distributions on $\mathbb{R}^d$ coincide if and only if all of their one-dimensional projections coincide. Matching the univariate marginals along a sufficient number of random directions is therefore enough to match the full joint distribution. The full objective is the sum of the prediction loss and a weighted SIGReg term. No stop-gradient, exponential moving average, or auxiliary stabilization heuristic is employed: gradients propagate through both terms, and all components are trained jointly end-to-end. The formal definitions of the encoder--predictor mappings, the prediction loss, and SIGReg are given in Appendix~\ref{app:a}.

\subsection{Layer-wise Probing}
\label{sec:probing}
To investigate how surgical information is distributed across the layers of each frozen encoder, this project employs layer-wise probing, following \textcite{joseph-2026}. Linear probes are used throughout, so that the layers in which phase information becomes linearly separable can be identified, rather than recovered through computation by the probe itself.

Let $f_\theta$ denote a frozen encoder of depth $L$ and let $h^{(\ell)}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{T_\ell \times d_\ell}$ denote the token-level activations it produces at layer $\ell$ for input frame $x$, where $T_\ell$ is the number of spatial tokens and $d_\ell$ the channel dimension. To obtain a fixed-size descriptor independent of spatial resolution, a pooling operator $\mathrm{pool}(\cdot)$ collapses the token axis,
\begin{equation}
z^{(\ell)}(x) = \mathrm{pool}\!\left(h^{(\ell)}(x)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}.
\end{equation}
The pooling operator is architecture-specific. For LeJEPA, $z^{(\ell)}$ is the mean over the patch tokens of the $\ell$-th transformer block, with the \texttt{[CLS]} token excluded so that the descriptor reflects spatial content rather than the global classification token. For GSViT, $z^{(\ell)}$ is the spatial mean over the $(H_\ell \times W_\ell)$ feature map produced by the patch-embedding layer or by one of the EfficientViT blocks; because EfficientViT widens its channel dimension across stages, $d_\ell$ grows with depth. For each layer $\ell$, a linear classifier
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}^{(\ell)}(x) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(W^{(\ell)} z^{(\ell)}(x) + b^{(\ell)}\right)
\end{equation}
is trained over the $C$ surgical phases of Cholec80 by minimising the cross-entropy
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(\ell)} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{(x, y) \in \mathcal{B}} \log \hat{y}^{(\ell)}_y(x)
\end{equation}
over mini-batches $\mathcal{B}$ with AdamW. The probe parameters $W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d_\ell}$ and $b^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ are the only trainable quantities; the encoder $f_\theta$ remains frozen, so a single forward pass over the evaluation set is sufficient and the cached features are reused across all hyperparameter configurations.

Probe quality is reported using three classification metrics: top-1 accuracy, macro $F_1$, and macro Jaccard. To ensure that probe scores reflect generalisation rather than memorisation of patient-specific cues, evaluation is performed under $5$-fold cross-validation grouped by video. Let $\mathcal{V}$ be the set of $80$ Cholec80 videos and $\{\mathcal{V}_k\}_{k=1}^{5}$ a partition with $\mathcal{V}_k \cap \mathcal{V}_{k'} = \emptyset$ for $k \neq k'$. Within fold $k$, all frames belonging to videos in $\mathcal{V}_k$ form the validation portion and the remainder forms the training portion, so no surgery contributes frames to both splits. A small grid search over learning rate and weight decay is performed inside each fold and the configuration achieving the highest top-1 accuracy on the validation portion is retained; the reported scores are the mean and sample standard deviation across the five folds. The specific values of the grid and optimizer are listed in the experimental setup.

\subsection{Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection}
\label{sec:umap}
To complement the layer-wise probing with a qualitative view of the learned representations, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection \parencite{mcinnes-2018} is used to project the per-layer embeddings of GSViT and LeJEPA into two dimensions. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection is preferred over t-distributed Stochastic Neighbour Embedding \parencite{van-der-maaten-2008} because it preserves the global structure of the embedding space more faithfully, allowing the relative positions of phase clusters to be interpreted alongside their internal coherence. The algorithm proceeds in two stages. In the first stage, it constructs a weighted $k$-nearest-neighbour graph on the cached features and interprets the edge weights as fuzzy membership strengths. For each point $x_i$, the distance $\rho_i$ to its nearest neighbour is treated as the local notion of zero distance, and a per-point scale $\sigma_i$ is set so that the membership weights of the $k$ nearest neighbours sum to $\log_2 k$. The directed weight from $x_i$ to a neighbour $x_j$ is
\begin{equation}
v_{j \mid i} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\max\!\left(0,\, d(x_i, x_j) - \rho_i\right)}{\sigma_i}\right),
\end{equation}
where $d(\cdot, \cdot)$ is the chosen metric, taken to be cosine in this work. Because $v_{j \mid i}$ and $v_{i \mid j}$ generally disagree, the directed graph is symmetrised through the fuzzy union
\begin{equation}
v_{ij} = v_{j \mid i} + v_{i \mid j} - v_{j \mid i}\, v_{i \mid j},
\end{equation}
yielding a single weight per undirected edge. In the second stage, a low-dimensional embedding $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{N} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is initialised by spectral embedding of the symmetrised graph and then optimized so that its own fuzzy graph matches the high-dimensional one. Membership in the low-dimensional space is parameterised by a smooth analogue of the high-dimensional kernel,
\begin{equation}
w_{ij} = \left(1 + a\, \|y_i - y_j\|_2^{2b}\right)^{-1},
\end{equation}
where the constants $a$ and $b$ are fitted to the chosen minimum-distance parameter so that points with low-dimensional separation below $\mathrm{min\_dist}$ receive near-maximal membership. The embedding is found by minimising the fuzzy cross-entropy
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{UMAP}} = \sum_{i \neq j} v_{ij} \log\frac{v_{ij}}{w_{ij}} + (1 - v_{ij}) \log\frac{1 - v_{ij}}{1 - w_{ij}}
\end{equation}
through stochastic gradient descent, with attractive updates applied along edges of the high-dimensional graph and repulsive updates applied to negatively sampled non-neighbour pairs. To control for the influence of random initialisation, the projection is run with multiple random seeds and the resulting embeddings are compared for consistency. The hyperparameters used are listed in the experimental setup.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
The experiments are designed to characterise how surgical information is distributed across the layers of GSViT and LeJEPA, and to compare the inductive biases that pixel-based and latent-based pretraining instil in each model.

\paragraph{Pretraining and Evaluation Datasets.} GSViT and LeJEPA are pretrained on the union of CATARACTS, JIGSAWS, and PitVis, capped at one million frames per dataset to balance procedural representation. Cholec80 is held out as the evaluation dataset; all $80$ videos are used for probing, with frames decoded at $1$~fps and resized to $224 \times 224$.

\paragraph{Probing Parameters.} The probing task is surgical phase recognition over the seven phases of the standard Cholec80 annotation. Each linear probe is optimized with AdamW for $20$ epochs at an inner batch size of $4{,}096$. The per-fold hyperparameter grid contains $5$ learning rates and $4$ weight-decay values, for $20$ configurations in total. Cross-validation uses $5$ folds grouped by video, and the absence of group leakage between training and validation portions is asserted at run time. The configuration achieving the highest top-1 accuracy on the held-out  fold is retained, and that fold's accuracy, macro $F_1$, and macro 
Jaccard are recorded. Accuracy alone is a poor summary statistic given 
Cholec80's skewed phase distribution, so macro $F_1$ and macro Jaccard 
are included to give equal weight to minority phases; their definitions 
and a fuller justification are given in the metrics paragraph below.

\paragraph{Evaluation Metrics.} Three metrics are reported for each 
linear probe. Top-1 accuracy measures the fraction of correctly 
classified frames,
\begin{equation}
    \text{Accuracy} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_i = y_i],
\end{equation}
but is a poor summary statistic for Cholec80 because the phase 
distribution is heavily skewed: \emph{CalotTriangleDissection} accounts 
for a disproportionate share of frames, so a classifier that concentrates 
capacity on the majority phase can achieve high accuracy while failing on 
clinically critical minority phases. Macro $F_1$ and macro Jaccard 
correct for this by averaging the per-class scores with equal weight 
across all $C = 7$ phases,
\begin{equation}
    \text{macro-}F_1 = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} 
    \frac{2\,\text{TP}_c}{2\,\text{TP}_c + \text{FP}_c + \text{FN}_c},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{macro-Jaccard} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} 
    \frac{\text{TP}_c}{\text{TP}_c + \text{FP}_c + \text{FN}_c},
\end{equation}
where $\text{TP}_c$, $\text{FP}_c$, and $\text{FN}_c$ are the true 
positives, false positives, and false negatives for phase $c$. 
Macro Jaccard is strictly lower than macro $F_1$ for any imperfect 
classifier, and is more sensitive to simultaneous false positives and 
false negatives, making it a conservative lower bound on recognition 
quality. Reporting all three metrics together allows accuracy gains that 
stem from majority-class exploitation to be distinguished from genuine 
improvements in per-phase discrimination.

\paragraph{Layer-wise UMAP Visualization Parameters.} For each model and each layer, a deterministic random subsample of up to $20{,}000$ frames is drawn from the full Cholec80 set (the same cache used by the linear probes) and projected with $n\_\text{neighbors} = 30$, $\mathrm{min\_dist} = 0.1$, cosine metric, and $n\_\text{components} = 2$. Each scatter is colored by the ground-truth phase label; no class information enters the projection itself, so any apparent structure reflects what the encoder has learned rather than what the visualiser has been told.


\paragraph{Implementation.} The code for the GSViT model was adapted based on the \textcite{schmidgall2024general} paper's provided code \footnote{https://github.com/SamuelSchmidgall/GSViT; The code included a bug in its deeper layers when it comes to its SE Attention modules, however this was fixed and documented.}. The LeJEPA model was adapted and changed based on the \textcite{maes-2026} paper's code \footnote{https://github.com/lucas-maes/le-wm; The original code used action-conditioned as its input however this was adapted to be complicable for this project.}. The full repository can be found on github \footnote{Full repository: https://github.com/MRSinal/thesis}.
All experiments are implemented in PyTorch, with \texttt{scikit-learn}
used for cross-validation and metric computation, \texttt{umap-learn}
for the UMAP projections, OpenCV for frame decoding, and Hydra for
configuration management. Pretraining is run on \textit{1 NVIDIA A40 GPU} for \textit{~20 hours} for GSViT and \textit{~35 hours} for LeJEPA. Probing and visualization are run on a single GPU. Per-layer feature tensors are cached to disk after the first extraction pass so that the probing sweeps and UMAP projections can be repeated without re-running the encoders. A single random seed governs the PyTorch RNG (and hence probe-head initialisation and the AdamW shuffle order); the data loader runs with \texttt{shuffle=False} and \texttt{GroupKFold} is deterministic, so the only remaining source of variance is across-fold sampling, which is absorbed into the standard deviations reported in the probing results.

\subsection{Pretraining}
Both encoders trained stably to convergence on the curated corpus, but did so along very different optimization trajectories and at different compute budgets
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pretrain}). Because the two objectives operate in
different spaces, an $L_1$ reconstruction loss in pixel space for
GSViT and an $L_2$ prediction loss in latent space combined with the
SIGReg regularizer for LeJEPA, the absolute loss magnitudes are not
directly comparable; the value of the curves lies in their shape and
stability rather than in cross-model comparison.

LeJEPA exhibits the characteristic JEPA training profile: a sharp
drop within the first few thousand updates, as the predictor and the
SIGReg-shaped embedding geometry co-adapt, followed by a long plateau
close to zero that is sustained for the remainder of training.
Crucially, no representation collapse is observed: the loss
stabilizes at a small but non-zero value rather than identically
zero, indicating that the encoder is not driving every input to a
constant vector \parencite{balestriero-2025}. This is achieved
without EMA teachers, stop-gradient updates, or any of the auxiliary
heuristics typically required by latent-based methods, supporting the
central claim of the LeJEPA framework that SIGReg alone is sufficient
to keep latent pretraining stable. GSViT shows a smooth,
near-monotonic decrease across $\sim$150k updates with no
instabilities, consistent with the well-behaved optimization profile
expected from a pixel reconstruction objective.

In terms of training efficiency, GSViT is the cheaper of the two

A40, an $\approx$$1.8\times$ overhead for the latent-prediction

components: a separate predictor transformer that consumes a window
of past embeddings, the projection heads attached to both the encoder
and the predictor, and the $M = 1024$ random projections required by
SIGReg at every step. GSViT, by contrast, performs only a single
forward pass through the EfficientViT encoder and its asymmetric
decoder. The extra cost incurred by LeJEPA is therefore the price
paid for operating in latent space and for the explicit anti-collapse
regularizer, rather than evidence of an inefficient training methodology.

\subsection{Layer-wise probing}
The probe profiles summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:probe_layerwise} already
establish that GSViT and LeJEPA reproduce the differences between
pixel-trained and latent-prediction encoders reported by
\textcite{joseph-2026}. Three further observations follow. First, LeJEPA leads GSViT at every depth after Layer 1 and on every metric, but the gap is markedly wider on the macro-averaged class-imbalance-aware metrics than on plain accuracy. Comparing each model at its own best layer, the
two differ by $3.7$\%\ on accuracy ($0.556$ vs.\ $0.519$), by $0.08$ on macro-$F_1$ ($0.321$ vs.\ $0.241$), and by $0.052$ on macro-Jaccard ($0.213$ vs.\
$0.161$). Cholec80 phase durations are heavily
skewed: \emph{CalotTriangleDissection} and \emph{GallbladderDissection}
dominate the frame count while \emph{ClippingCutting} and
\emph{GallbladderPackaging} occupy short windows, so accuracy can be
carried largely by majority-class predictions, whereas macro-$F_1$ and
Jaccard penalize neglect of the rarer phases. The disproportionately
larger LeJEPA advantage on the macro metrics therefore indicates not
just that its features are more linearly separable on average, but that
it places the minority phases on a more linearly separable manifold than
GSViT's. The contrast is sharpest at the final layer of each model:
GSViT collapses to $F_1 = 0.167 \pm 0.037$ and Jaccard $= 0.112 \pm 0.023$,
while LeJEPA still retains $F_1 = 0.251 \pm 0.054$ and Jaccard $= 0.167
\pm 0.032$. This is the property a latent-prediction objective with explicit
anti-collapse regularization is designed to encourage
\parencite{balestriero-2025}: by spreading embeddings to match an
isotropic Gaussian rather than reconstructing pixels, LeJEPA has no
in-built incentive to allocate more capacity to common phases.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Findings.} This thesis set out to assess whether a latent-predictive pretraining objective improves the semantic robustness and topological organization of surgical phase representations relative to a pixel-reconstruction objective. Both sub-questions are answered affirmatively. On Sub-Question~1, LeJEPA leads GSViT at every depth and on every metric, with the gap widening sharply on the class-imbalance-aware macro $F_1$ ($\sim$33\% relative) and macro Jaccard ($\sim$32\% relative) compared to plain accuracy ($\sim$7\% relative). Most of LeJEPA's layers beats GSViT's best layer on macro-$F_1$, indicating that pixel reconstruction structurally under-allocates capacity to the rare phases that SIGReg's isotropic-Gaussian constraint protects. Because the isotropic-Gaussian 
prior penalizes representational collapse in any direction, rare phases  and atypical frame appearances receive as much capacity as dominant ones, a property that matters disproportionately in surgical video where inter-patient variability is high and minority phases such as \emph{ClippingCutting} carry the highest clinical stakes. On Sub-Question~2, the UMAP projections corroborate this picture: GSViT remains a near-amorphous cloud at every depth, while LeJEPA develops progressively richer, phase-coherent manifold structure that tracks the probe-quality ranking. The mid-depth emergence zone (layers 7--9), where probe means peak and cross-fold variance is smallest, replicates the latent-vs-pixel contrast reported by \textcite{joseph-2026}, and supports the claim of \textcite{balestriero-2025} that SIGReg alone, without EMA teachers or stop-gradients, suffices to keep latent pretraining stable under domain shift. The specific contribution is thus a layer-resolved, side-by-side comparison of pixel-based and latent-based foundation models in surgery, trained under matched data and compute budgets, suggesting that a substantial share of the gains UniSurg \parencite{wu-2026} attributes to its V-JEPA-style cascade of heuristics may stem from the latent objective itself.

\paragraph{Limitation.} Several limitations temper these conclusions. The pretraining corpus is 5x smaller than UniSurg's, only Cholec80 phase recognition is used for evaluation, both encoders are probed in a frozen single-frame mode that discards exactly the temporal information LeJEPA is trained to predict. The static-frame protocol also raises a sharper interpretive question that this study cannot settle: the UMAP branches do not consistently follow the canonical phase ordering, suggesting that LeJEPA may lean on spatial cues (instrument identity, anatomical view, tissue appearance) to forecast the next embedding rather than encoding genuinely temporal structure. Future work should therefore pursue three directions. First, attention-rollout maps over both encoders would convert the present geometric argument into a perceptual one, making it directly inspectable whether LeJEPA attends to instrument--tissue interaction zones while GSViT spreads attention over semantically uninformative regions such as specular highlights and smoke. Second, evaluating the frozen LeJEPA encoder on time-reversed Cholec80 clips, and symmetrically perturbing GSViT's temporal context at probing time, would localize how much of each model's semantics is genuinely temporal versus spatial. Third, scaling the comparison along corpus size, predictor capacity, and downstream tasks beyond phase recognition (tool presence, triplets, gesture recognition) would establish whether the observed gap is fixed or a function of each objective's scaling law, and whether the late-layer drop deepens with predictor capacity as the predictor-specialization hypothesis predicts.
\section{Conclusion}

This thesis investigated the extent to which a latent-predictive pretraining objective improves the semantic robustness and topological organization of surgical phase representations relative to a pixel-reconstruction objective. To answer this, GSViT and LeJEPA were pretrained under matched data and compute budgets on a curated corpus of CATARACTS, JIGSAWS, and PitVis, and their frozen representations were probed layer by layer on Cholec80 phase recognition and visualized with UMAP. The key takeaway is that the choice of pretraining objective dominates over architectural sophistication or training scale in shaping what surgical foundation models actually learn. LeJEPA's latent-prediction objective, regularized only by SIGReg, produces representations that are more linearly separable, more topologically organized, and substantially more equitable across rare and common phases than those produced by GSViT's pixel-reconstruction objective, with the latter failing to surface phase semantics in any geometry, linear or otherwise. The mid-depth emergence zone observed in LeJEPA replicates, in the surgical domain, the latent-vs-pixel distinction previously reported only on natural video, and the stability of LeJEPA's training without EMA teachers or stop-gradients confirms that the minimal-recipe JEPA framework transfers to a small, sensitive, domain-shifted corpus.

These findings carry three implications. For the field of surgical computer vision, they suggest that the engineering complexity that has accumulated around latent surgical foundation models, motion-guided masking, spatiotemporal self-distillation, feature-diversity regularizers, may be doing less work than the underlying latent objective itself, and that future surgical foundation models should be built around minimal latent-prediction recipes before reaching for auxiliary heuristics. For practice, the disproportionate macro-$F_1$ advantage of latent features on minority phases matters clinically, since transitions in and out of short phases are precisely where workflow-aware decision support is most useful; pretraining objective choice is therefore not a neutral implementation detail but a determinant of which surgical events a downstream system can reliably recognize. For theory, the persistence of the emergence-zone phenomenon under a fundamentally different visual distribution strengthens the case that this is a property of the objective rather than of the natural-image domain, and that latent-predictive pretraining produces a characteristic representational geometry, mid-depth peak, manifold branching, isotropic spread across classes, that is reproducible across domains. Whether this geometry is genuinely temporal or merely a spatial artefact of a temporal objective remains the central open question that this work raises and that future probing under temporal perturbation, attention rollout, and broader downstream evaluation will need to answer.

\subsection*{GSViT Architectural Details}

This section provides the formal definitions of the EfficientViT components used in GSViT.

\paragraph{Sandwich layout.} The sandwich layout reduces the number of self-attention layers in a block by surrounding a single self-attention layer with multiple MLP layers. Let $\Phi_i^{\mathrm{A}}$ denote the self-attention layer of the $i$-th block and $\Phi_i^{\mathrm{F}}$ an MLP layer. With $N$ MLPs applied before and after the attention layer, the block can be written as
\begin{equation}
X_{i+1} = \prod_{i}^{N} \Phi_i^{\mathrm{F}}\!\left(\Phi_i^{\mathrm{A}}\!\left(\prod_{i}^{N} \Phi_i^{\mathrm{F}}(X_i)\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where $X_i$ is the input feature of the $i$-th block and $X_{i+1}$ its output. Because self-attention is computationally more expensive than the MLP layers it is interleaved with, this layout yields a more memory- and time-efficient block.

\paragraph{Cascaded Group Attention.} CGA splits the input feature $X_i$ along the channel dimension into $h$ disjoint groups $\{X_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{h}$ and routes each group to a separate attention head. Each head computes
\begin{equation}
\tilde{X}_{ij} = \mathrm{Attn}\!\left(X_{ij} W^{Q}_{ij},\, X_{ij} W^{K}_{ij},\, X_{ij} W^{V}_{ij}\right),
\end{equation}
with per-head projections $W^{Q}_{ij}, W^{K}_{ij}, W^{V}_{ij}$. The head outputs are concatenated and projected to form the block output:
\begin{equation}
X_{i+1} = \mathrm{Concat}\!\left[\tilde{X}_{ij}\right]_{j=1}^{h} W^{P}_{i}.
\end{equation}
Within a block, the heads are arranged cascadingly: the output of head $j-1$ is added to the input of head $j$,
\begin{equation}
X'_{ij} = X_{ij} + \tilde{X}_{i(j-1)}, \qquad 1 < j \leq h,
\end{equation}
so that later heads refine the representation produced by earlier ones, increasing the effective depth of the attention stack without introducing additional parameters.

\subsection*{LeJEPA Architectural Details}

This section provides the formal definitions of the LeJEPA architecture and training objective.

\paragraph{Encoder and predictor.} Given a frame $\mathrm{f}_t$, the encoder produces a latent representation
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{z}_t = enc_\theta(\mathrm{f}_t),
\end{equation}
and the predictor forecasts the embedding of the next frame from a window of $N$ past embeddings:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{\hat{z}}_{t+1} = pred_\phi(\mathrm{z}_{t-N+1},\,\dots,\,\mathrm{z}_t).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Prediction loss.} The prediction loss is computed under teacher forcing as the squared Euclidean distance between the predictor's output and the encoder's embedding of the actual next frame:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{pred} \triangleq \lVert \mathrm{\hat{z}}_{t+1} - \mathrm{z}_{t+1} \rVert_2^2.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{SIGReg.} Let $\mathrm{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times B \times d}$ denote the tensor of latent embeddings collected over a history of length $N$, a batch of size $B$, and an embedding dimension $d$. Testing for Gaussianity directly in $d$ dimensions is impractical, since classical normality tests are formulated for univariate samples and degrade as the dimensionality grows. SIGReg side-steps this limitation by working through random one-dimensional projections: it samples $M$ unit-norm directions $\mathrm{u}^{(m)} \in \mathbb{S}^{d-1}$, projects the embeddings onto each direction to obtain $\mathrm{h}^{(m)} = \mathrm{Z}\mathrm{u}^{(m)}$, and applies the univariate Epps--Pulley statistic $T(\cdot)$ to each projection. By the Cram\'er--Wold theorem, two distributions on $\mathbb{R}^d$ coincide if and only if all of their one-dimensional projections coincide, so matching all univariate marginals is sufficient to match the full joint distribution. The regularizer is the average of the resulting statistics:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SIGReg}(\mathrm{Z}) \triangleq \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} T(\mathrm{h}^{(m)}).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Full objective.} The complete LeJEPA objective combines the prediction loss and the SIGReg regularizer with weight $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{LeJEPA} \triangleq \mathcal{L}_{pred} + \lambda \, \mathrm{SIGReg}(\mathrm{Z}).
\end{equation}
Following the defaults reported in \parencite{balestriero-2025}, the project adopts $M = 1024$ and $\lambda = 0.1$. Gradients propagate through both terms and the encoder, predictor, and projection heads are optimized jointly end-to-end.

\section{UMAP Details} \label{app:b}

UMAP \parencite{mcinnes-2018} constructs a weighted $k$-nearest-neighbor graph over the high-dimensional embeddings $\{\mathrm{X}_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and learns a low-dimensional layout $\{\mathrm{y}_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ whose own neighbourhood graph matches the high-dimensional one as closely as possible. The high-dimensional similarities are defined as
\begin{equation}
p_{j|i} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\max(0,\, \lVert \mathrm{X}_i - \mathrm{X}_j \rVert_2 - \rho_i)}{\sigma_i}\right), \qquad p_{ij} = p_{j|i} + p_{i|j} - p_{j|i}\, p_{i|j},
\end{equation}
where $\rho_i$ is the distance to the nearest neighbour of $\mathrm{X}_i$ and $\sigma_i$ is set so that the effective neighbourhood size matches a user-specified value $k$. The low-dimensional similarities take the form
\begin{equation}
q_{ij} = \left(1 + a\,\lVert \mathrm{y}_i - \mathrm{y}_j \rVert_2^{2b}\right)^{-1},
\end{equation}
with $(a, b)$ fitted to a smooth approximation of a piecewise function controlled by the minimum-distance hyperparameter. The layout is then optimized by minimizing the cross-entropy between the two graphs:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{UMAP} = \sum_{i \neq j} \left[ p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}} + (1 - p_{ij}) \log \frac{1 - p_{ij}}{1 - q_{ij}} \right].
\end{equation}
The first term acts as an attractive force between points that are close in the high-dimensional graph, while the second term acts as a repulsive force between points that are not.

The project uses the \texttt{umap-learn} implementation with $k = 30$ nearest neighbours, a minimum distance of $0.1$, and the cosine metric, with the remaining optimization settings left at the library defaults of \textcite{mcinnes-2018}. The cosine metric is chosen because the semantic content of transformer representations is carried by direction rather than magnitude, so that similarity between embeddings reflects orientation in feature space rather than per-layer norm differences. Embeddings are extracted from the output of each transformer block and projected into two dimensions using a fixed random seed, with up to $20{,}000$ frames drawn as a deterministic subsample to keep the optimization tractable. Visualizations are colored by surgical phase to assess the degree to which each model's representation aligns with surgically meaningful structure.

\section{Pretraining Diagnostics} \label{app:c}

Following the reporting convention of \textcite{schmidgall2024general}, 
the GSViT pretraining loss is reported after Exponential Moving Average 
(EMA) smoothing, defined as $\text{EMA}_t = \alpha \cdot x_t + (1 - 
\alpha) \cdot \text{EMA}_{t-1}$, where $x_t$ is the raw per-step loss 
and $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ controls the smoothing window. Figure~\ref{fig:gsvit_curves} shows the same pixel-space $L_1$ reconstruction loss at two smoothing scales. The short-term view ($\alpha = 0.98$) retains more step-to-step variation and shows a sharp initial drop from approximately $0.6$ to below $0.1$ within the first 10k steps. The long-term view ($\alpha = 0.997$) filters out short-term fluctuations and confirms a smooth, near-monotonic decrease across the full 180k steps with no instabilities or divergences. The agreement between both smoothing scales indicates that the downward trend is a stable property of the optimization rather than an artifact of individual batch variation.
